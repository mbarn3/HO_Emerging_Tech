An investigation into the emerging technologies used by the Home Office in the asylum process.

Lizzie, Genie, Matt, Mattie, Molly

The UK Home Office's use of emerging technologies in the asylum system. 



Britain's asylum system has faced increased pressure for years. In the year leading up to March 2026, more than 93,000 people were included in asylum applications in the UK, while tens of thousands continued to wait for decisions on their claims. The backlog of unresolved cases and lengthy processing times has placed increasing demands on the Home Office and local authorities.

As the pressure on the system has grown, so too has interest in technological solutions.

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being presented as a way to speed up decision-making and reduce costs. But are these new technologies actually improving the efficiency, or simply introducing new risks into an already complex system in the name of cost-efficiency?

Known in court documents only as IA, a young asylum seeker arrived in the UK claiming to be 16 years old. The Home Office instead assessed him as a 23-year-old man, denying him the protections normally given to children in the asylum system. Only after legal proceedings did the government accept that IA was, in fact, a child.

Whenever age verification of Asylum Seekers is mentioned, both in Government publications and news articles, the means are justified by the goal of separating adults and children for the purpose of accommodation. We do not dispute the need for secure accommodation for unaccompanied minors, but this statement often serves to shut down any conversation around the ethics of determining age becoming one of the default immediate processes after Asylum Seekers' often long, traumatic journeys.

The traumatic situations are a reality no matter what age, however the government imposes drastically different legal outcomes depending on what age they are categorized as.

If the system to determine whether a person is a minor or not is openly flawed with massive consequences, it makes you question how and why these technologies, which only amplify their failings, are being developed.

Facial Age Estimation (FAE), a form of artificial intelligence that attempts to estimate a person's age from an image of their face, is now being explored as part of the UK's asylum system. Unlike facial recognition software, which attempts to match a face to an identity, facial age estimation makes a judgment based on probability. Using large datasets of faces linked to known ages, algorithms attempt to determine whether someone is likely to be above or below a certain age threshold.

For immigration officials dealing with age disputes, the technology promises speed and cost- efficiency.

Under the Children Act 1989, unaccompanied asylum-seeking children are legally recognised as “children in need”, placing safeguarding responsibilities on local authorities. They are entitled to accommodation, protection, and support. In March this year, the UK government reduced the refugee permission to stay for adults and accompanied children to 30 months, whereas unaccompanied children continue to have a 5-year permission to stay. Determining whether someone is a child, therefore, carries major legal and financial consequences.

So, one of the first questions asked of anyone who arrives in the UK is “what age are you?”. Lack of documentation is common. A judgment, without using a translator, is made by a migration officer based on physical appearance and demeanour, who, prior to 2023, had no training in assessing age. A new 2-day training scheme for migration officers has already raised concerns over its quality, especially with respect to culture-specific ageing and the effects of trauma on ageing. Notoriously culturally incompetent, immigration officers have the power to dispute the claimed age of an asylum seeker.

If there is a conflict between the claimed age and the initial age assessment, the age dispute moves to the local authorities.

Since the 2003 B v Merton ruling, local authorities have relied on detailed “Merton-compliant” assessments carried out by trained social workers, with safeguarding designed for children in place. However, in 2021, the Home Office, under a Conservative government, started looking for scientific alternatives.

The government established the Age Estimation Science Advisory Committee (AESAC), bringing together experts in paediatrics, dentistry, child safeguarding, social work, radiology, genetics and statistics to advise on scientifically based methods of age assessment. Within the committee's reports, there was strong recognition that no method could perfectly determine a person's age. One early report concluded that “there is no infallible method” capable of producing an exact match to chronological age.

On a parallel timeline, the Home Office was exploring facial age estimation technology through separate trials and pilot schemes.

In 2021, the technology company Yoti was awarded a Home Office proof-of-concept contract related to age estimation. The following year, the Home Office carried out nine trials involving facial age estimation systems in retail settings, based on the Challenge 25 scheme. While the advisory committee was tasked with evaluating both the scientific and ethical challenges of emerging technologies, meeting records suggest many members felt increasingly excluded from discussions surrounding FAE.

By 2024, committee members were openly questioning government representatives about plans to use facial age estimation at the UK border. Internal minutes describe concern that the technology was being considered as a “quick method” for assessing age despite the lack of sufficient evidence surrounding its accuracy in asylum contexts.

In an informal meeting in June 2025, the group shared their discontentment with the Home Office's move towards FAE and being excluded from related discussions. Why were specialists in relevant fields such as biological signs of ageing, child protection and statistics kept away from the topic?

A month later, in July 2025, AESAC was shut down. That same month, the Minister of State for Border Security and Asylum announced that the Home Office would be pushing forward with FAE, as it was “the most cost-effective option”.

AESAC's statistician, Tim Cole, requested to review an unpublished Home Office report on their FAE tests, involving more than 6,000 individuals. His request was ignored, and the report went unpublished. Yet, according to Cole's analysis, more than half of the 17-year-olds tested by the highest-performing algorithm would have been incorrectly classified as adults. For asylum seekers, the consequences of such errors are profound.

A child wrongly identified as an adult may be placed in adult accommodation, lose access to safeguarding protections and navigate the asylum process without specialist support. Cases like IA's demonstrate how heavily the system already relies on subjective assessments of appearance and behaviour. Facial age estimation attempts to automate those same judgements through artificial intelligence.

The Home Office is also taking steps to integrate AI into the caseworking systems for asylum cases.

Asylum Case Summarisation (ACS) and Asylum Policy Search (APS) are AI tools developed for the Home Office by Methods Analytics sometime prior to May 2024. ACS uses ChatGPT-4 to summarise asylum interview transcripts, while APS is an AI search assistant that finds and summarises country policy information.

Between May and December of 2024, the tools were tested by asylum caseworkers at the Home Office. The subsequent evaluation of the trials found that for APS, 54% of survey respondents reported they would continue using the tool, and 79% felt confident in the tool's accuracy. With ACS, only 42% of participants felt it gave them the right amount of information, 9% of summaries produced were deemed to be so inaccurate that they had to be removed from the pilot. Of the summaries that progressed in the pilot, 23% of users reported they were not fully confident in the summary information.

The report also states that “The tools did not appear to impact negatively or positively on decision quality”. However, decision quality was assessed using Calibre, a Home Office assessment tool described by UNHCR as appearing to overlook “a number of important quality issues”.

In response to the Home Office's use of this AI technology, Open Rights Group commissioned a legal opinion. The case study found that the use of ACS and APS is likely to be unlawful and does not follow the Government's own UK AI Playbook. One issue they highlighted was that if asylum seekers are not informed that AI is being used to determine their claims, which an FOI request from July 2025 states is the case, this is likely to be unlawful.

They also found that the Home Office's evaluation of the ACS and APS trials does not provide an adequate assessment of the impact on decision quality. Thanks to the evaluation's use of Calibre, they only tested decision quality by seeing if a similar proportion of decisions were unchallenged in the test and control group, not the extent to which the tools correctly summarised the information. The legal opinion also highlights that the evaluation does not provide an adequate assessment of discrimination, with the evaluation itself stating that "pilot numbers were too small to fully test for variation in outcome for all characteristics".

The Home Office evidently does not see these failings as a problem, as in 2025, for their work on the APS and ACS evaluation, the Home Office AI Analysis Team were shortlisted for a Civil Service Evaluation and Analysis Award.

Just like facial age estimation, the APS and ACS evaluation repeatedly places the time-saving benefits of the technology above any mention of inaccuracies. These oversights by the Home Office again highlight the trend of rushing out technology that is not fit for purpose, with the sole aim of reducing the backlog and saving money. Both technologies were planned for rollout at the start of this year, with APS available to all caseworkers, and ACS possibly also in operational use.

While the use of AI in asylum procedures remains controversial, similar systems are quietly becoming part of everyday life. The questions raised by these technologies extend beyond immigration policy.

Supporters argue these systems are more privacy-friendly than traditional identification checks because they do not require individuals to share official documents. Critics question whether they normalise a form of algorithmic profiling in which access to services is determined by a machine's interpretation of someone's appearance.

The expansion of facial age estimation reflects a broader pattern in the development of surveillance and identification technologies. Historically, border systems and immigration controls have served as testing grounds for new forms of biometric monitoring, automated decision-making, and digital verification. As Catherine Besteman observes in Militarized Global Apartheid, this process contributes to “an emergent new world order in which race and mobility feature as primary variables for which heightened security and militarization are the answer.” Technologies initially justified within governmental contexts are tested on marginalised populations, often entering mainstream use once they are normalised, illustrating a recurring trajectory of technological adoption.

Facial recognition followed a similar trajectory. Initially associated with policing and national security, it is now used to unlock phones, verify online identities and monitor public spaces. Facial age estimation appears to be following the same path, moving from the border into ordinary interactions between individuals, governments and corporations.

If algorithms can estimate age from a face today, what other characteristics might governments and companies attempt to infer tomorrow? How much authority should automated systems hold in decisions involving rights, access and identity? And when those systems make mistakes, who is held accountable?

The debate is no longer simply about whether artificial intelligence can estimate age accurately. It is about what happens when governments prioritise speed and efficiency over expert advice in decisions that can fundamentally alter a child's future. For young people like IA, the consequences of getting age wrong are not theoretical. The question is whether facial age estimation will improve those decisions or simply automate their mistakes?

Since this article was written, the Home Office released two documents outlining their plans to roll out FAE in 2027 within border control.

Their statements acknowledge the unreliability of the technology, yet despite its lack of effectiveness, government officials have decided to move forward with its use anyway, allowing for a “margin of error”. However, this is a decision taken too lightly, considering the real-life repercussions such technology will bring to peoples' lives.
